# 《Why Sleep Debt Cannot Be Repaid in One Night》Knowledge Cards (6)

> Source: tests/samples/06-english.md | Knowledge points: 6 | Cards: 6

### Card 1 | Acute sleep loss and chronic restriction have different recovery profiles

**Core idea**: One recovery night largely restores attention after a single all-nighter, but not after weeks of short sleep.

**Explanation**: After one all-nighter, attention-task performance recovers substantially after one recovery night and approaches baseline after two. After two weeks at six hours a night, controlled studies show attention deficits equivalent to 24 to 48 hours of total sleep deprivation — on the same measure, far more recovery time is required. The two conditions are not interchangeable in kind, only in degree.

**Example / self-test**: The article's contrast — one night for acute loss, versus deficits that survive a single recovery night under chronic restriction.

### Card 2 | Subjective sleepiness adapts faster than objective impairment

**Core idea**: Chronically restricted sleepers feel nearly normal while performing as if severely deprived.

**Explanation**: Under two weeks of six-hour nights, participants' self-rated sleepiness barely changed even as their attention deficits reached the equivalent of 24 to 48 hours without sleep. The article calls this the core finding behind "sleep debt": adaptation happens in how you feel, not in how you perform.

**Example / self-test**:
Q: Why is "I feel fine" weak evidence that a short-sleep schedule is harmless?
A: Self-rated sleepiness adapts while measured attention deficits persist.

### Card 3 | Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors rather than clearing adenosine

**Core idea**: Caffeine restores alertness without reducing the underlying sleep debt.

**Explanation**: Adenosine accumulates as a byproduct of neural metabolism during wakefulness and is cleared during sleep; it binds to receptors that raise sleep pressure. Caffeine blocks those receptors, so alertness returns while the adenosine itself remains — the debt is masked, not discharged.

**Example / self-test**: The article's own formulation: caffeine restores alertness without discharging the debt, because the adenosine is still there.

### Card 4 | Recovery is asymmetric: attention recovers before metabolism

**Core idea**: Vigilance rebounds quickly after restriction; metabolic effects outlast cognitive ones.

**Explanation**: Attention and vigilance recover relatively fast, whereas memory consolidation and glucose metabolism lag. In one widely cited protocol, five nights restricted to four hours produced impaired insulin sensitivity that persisted after two nights of unrestricted recovery sleep.

**Example / self-test**: Five nights at four hours, then two recovery nights — insulin sensitivity was still impaired.

### Card 5 | The debt metaphor breaks down because sleep has no fixed exchange rate

**Core idea**: Extra sleep yields diminishing returns, and weekend catch-up can cancel part of its own benefit.

**Explanation**: The article treats "debt" as a limited metaphor: extending sleep beyond habitual duration has diminishing returns, and weekend recovery sleep shifts circadian timing later. The resulting "social jetlag" partially offsets the gain, so more sleep does not translate proportionally into more recovery.

**Example / self-test**:
Q: Why can sleeping in on weekends recover less than the hours suggest?
A: It shifts circadian timing later, producing social jetlag that cancels part of the benefit.

### Card 6 | People cannot reliably estimate their own impairment

**Core idea**: Individual vulnerability varies widely and is largely invisible to the person affected.

**Explanation**: Genetic factors affect both baseline sleep need and vulnerability to restriction, and self-assessment is poor. In Psychomotor Vigilance Task studies, restricted participants consistently rated themselves as functioning better than their measured performance indicated.

**Example / self-test**: The PVT finding — restricted participants rated their functioning above their measured performance.
